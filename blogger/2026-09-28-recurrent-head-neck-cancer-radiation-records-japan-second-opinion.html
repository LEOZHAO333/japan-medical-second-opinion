I Was Told I Cannot Have More Radiotherapy. What Should I Send for a Japan Second Opinion?

Short answer

If recurrent head and neck cancer develops inside or near a previously irradiated area, a useful second opinion requires more than a discharge summary or a sentence saying “radiotherapy completed.” Japanese specialists need to know exactly where the recurrent tumor is, what nearby organs previously received, and whether the current problem is mainly local or part of wider disease.

The most important missing item is often the original radiotherapy plan. Without dose maps and DICOM-RT data, technical assessment of re-irradiation or BNCT may be limited.

Why the previous radiation record matters

Head and neck tumors can lie close to major vessels, nerves, the jaw, airway and swallowing structures. Decisions therefore depend on cumulative dose, time since treatment, recurrence location and the likely value of local control.

“No more radiotherapy” may mean the risk is unacceptable, essential files are missing, another treatment is preferred, or local treatment is unlikely to change the overall outcome. A second opinion should identify which applies.

The six-part record package

  1. Pathology and proof of recurrence. Include the original pathology report, immunohistochemistry, any molecular testing, and the report or biopsy confirming recurrence. Treatment-related tissue change can sometimes resemble viable tumor, so pathology review may be relevant.
  2. Current imaging in original format. Provide recent contrast-enhanced CT or MRI and, when performed, PET-CT. Screenshots and written reports are not substitutes for the original DICOM images.
  3. Complete radiotherapy files. Request the treatment summary, planning CT, structure set, dose plan, dose-volume histogram and DICOM-RT export. If more than one radiation course was given, collect every course.
  4. A dated treatment timeline. List operations, radiotherapy dates, systemic drugs, treatment responses, major toxicities and the date recurrence was first suspected or confirmed.
  5. Current clinical status. Include recent blood tests, organ function, nutrition, swallowing and airway status, current medications and performance status as documented by the treating team.
  6. Three to five decision questions. Examples include: Is the recurrence technically resectable? Is any form of re-irradiation reasonable? Does Japan’s approved BNCT indication apply? Is systemic treatment the higher priority? Would an in-person visit change the decision?

Where BNCT fits—and where it does not

In Japan, borofalan-based accelerator BNCT is an approved treatment for unresectable locally advanced or locally recurrent head and neck cancer. Approval does not mean that every patient with recurrent disease is eligible. Tumor location and depth, proximity to major vessels, the treatment center’s technical criteria, prior therapy and overall disease burden all matter.

BNCT for brain tumors and many other cancers must be labelled separately according to its actual stage, such as a clinical trial, clinical research or individual use. It should not be presented as routine approved care simply because BNCT is approved for a defined head and neck indication.

When a remote written review is useful

A remote review can help when hospitals disagree or when surgery, systemic therapy, precision radiotherapy and BNCT need comparison. It may confirm the local plan, request missing files or explain why treatment in Japan is unlikely to add value.

Major bleeding, breathing difficulty, rapidly worsening swallowing problems, severe infection or altered consciousness require urgent local medical care. An overseas opinion should not delay emergency treatment.

What to do next

Ask the previous radiation oncology department—not only the medical records desk—for the complete treatment planning export. Keep a copy of the original files and share them only through a secure, access-controlled process. Do not post medical records or identity documents in public comments.

A Japan expert second opinion is a decision-support tool. It does not guarantee BNCT eligibility, treatment acceptance or a recommendation different from the current team, and it does not automatically require travel to Japan.

FAQ

Is a radiotherapy summary letter enough?

Usually not for a technical re-irradiation assessment. The summary is useful, but dose maps, dose-volume information and DICOM-RT files may be necessary.

Can someone who already had radiotherapy still be assessed for BNCT?

Yes, assessment is possible, but prior treatment alone does not establish eligibility. The recurrence location, cumulative dose, normal-tissue risk and the approved indication must be reviewed.

Does a second opinion mean the first hospital was wrong?

No. Different recommendations may reflect different treatment goals, available technology or risk tolerance. Confirmation of the current plan is also a valid result.

Should the patient travel to Japan before the records are reviewed?

Not necessarily. A written review can first clarify whether an in-person examination, additional testing or treatment in Japan is likely to be useful.

Can public response rates predict an individual outcome?

No. Study results describe defined patient groups and cannot predict an individual result. Eligibility, expected benefit and risk require specialist review.

Japan Expert Second Opinion

Define one concrete decision question and assemble the records needed to answer it. A Japan review may clarify pathology, imaging, prior radiation exposure and sequencing. Discuss the opinion with the local medical team.
