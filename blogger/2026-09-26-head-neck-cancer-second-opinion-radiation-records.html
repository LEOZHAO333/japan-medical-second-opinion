Before a Second Opinion for Recurrent Head and Neck Cancer, Ask for These Radiation Records

Direct answer: If a relative has recurrent head and neck cancer and has been told that another course of radiation may be unsafe, request more than the previous treatment summary. The treating radiation oncology department may need to supply the original radiation plan, dose distribution and dose–volume information, alongside recent scans and pathology. A specialist can then evaluate what was treated, where the new disease lies and which normal tissues may already have received substantial exposure. This checklist helps organize a second opinion; it does not establish whether any particular patient can receive further radiation.

Why a discharge letter may not be enough

“Thirty-five sessions of radiation” describes a schedule, not the dose delivered to every structure near the tumor. Head and neck treatment can involve areas close to major vessels, the spinal cord, jaw, swallowing muscles, salivary glands and airway. When disease returns near the original field, both the new tumor and tissue previously treated matter. The specialist may need to review the former plan and the current scan together before discussing surgery, systemic treatment, focused re-irradiation or another local option.

Some patients hear “no more radiation” after a reasonable assessment by their local team. A second opinion can confirm that decision. In other cases, the phrase refers to one technique, one proposed field or incomplete access to older dose files. Neither situation can be resolved from a single screenshot or a headline about a new device.

Ask the radiation clinic for four things

  1. Treatment summary. Request the treatment dates, hospital, treated site, prescription dose, number of fractions, technique and any interruption or significant toxicity. Include every previous course, not only the most recent one.
  2. Planning and dose files. Ask whether the clinic can export DICOM-RT data, including planning CT, structures, plan and dose objects, plus a dose–volume histogram when available. The receiving specialist should tell the family which formats can actually be reviewed. A PDF summary is useful but may not substitute for the original plan.
  3. Current imaging. Obtain the latest contrast-enhanced CT or MRI and, if used, PET-CT in the original format as well as the radiologist’s reports. Earlier comparison scans can clarify how the finding changed over time.
  4. Pathology and treatment timeline. Keep the original diagnosis, any biopsy of the recurrence, surgery and drug history together in a dated one-page timeline. The US National Cancer Institute notes that medical records and imaging are part of obtaining a second opinion, and pathology review may also matter.

If files cannot be exported immediately, ask the hospital what it can provide and whether direct clinic-to-clinic transfer is possible. Record the missing items. Do not upload scans, reports or identity documents in a public comment.

Where does BNCT fit?

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a form of radiotherapy that uses a boron-containing drug followed by neutron irradiation. In Japan, a borofalan-based approach is approved for a specified indication involving unresectable locally advanced or locally recurrent head and neck cancer. That regulatory status is approved treatment for the stated Japanese indication, not a general approval for every recurrence, for brain tumors or for all patients who previously received radiation. Japanese drug information and the treating center’s current criteria should be checked before describing an individual case as eligible.

Prior radiation neither automatically qualifies nor automatically excludes someone from a BNCT assessment. Tumor position, the exposed tissues, overall disease burden, performance status and center-specific evaluation can change the discussion. Work in other tumor types may be a clinical trial, research or individual use; those labels must not be collapsed into the approved head and neck indication.

Three questions to send with the records

  • Does the current imaging show a localized recurrence that could change the value of a local intervention?
  • What do the original radiation files reveal about previous dose to tissues now at risk?
  • Which choices should be compared now, and which could be carried out near home?

For heavy bleeding, difficulty breathing, rapidly worsening swallowing or another acute problem, seek urgent care locally. An overseas written opinion is not an emergency service.

Frequently asked questions

Can I request the original radiation plan years later?

Ask the hospital or radiation department that delivered treatment. Availability and export formats vary; tell the second-opinion team what is missing rather than assuming a summary reconstructs the plan.

Will a specialist decide from the DICOM-RT files alone?

No. Current imaging, pathology, previous treatments and the person's current condition are also needed. The files help reconstruct past exposure; they do not provide a complete treatment decision by themselves.

Does “no more radiation” mean BNCT is the next step?

No. BNCT is itself radiotherapy. A specialist must check the Japanese indication and the specific tumor, anatomy and safety questions before suggesting evaluation at a treatment center.

Must a second opinion lead to travel to Japan?

No. A written assessment may confirm the existing plan, suggest further review or identify a specific reason for an in-person assessment. It cannot guarantee a different plan or treatment acceptance.
